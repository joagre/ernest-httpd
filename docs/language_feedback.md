28. **At the limit of open files `Tcp.accept` answers at once, with no connection waiting, and E.18 does not say so.** E.18 has `accept` wait for a connection within its milliseconds, and §12 says that an operation that would open a file beyond the limit answers `Left(Other("too many open files"))`. Under `ulimit -n 64`, a program that opens listeners until one is refused, 47 of them, and then calls `Tcp.accept(first, 2000)` with nobody connecting is answered `Left(Other("too many open files"))` after 0 ms; with three listeners closed, the same call answers `Left(Timeout)` after 2001 ms. The host takes the descriptor before it waits for a connection.

    So an acceptor that asks again at once spins at the limit, and a server's `cannot accept` comes as its table fills, before any connection is turned away. ernest-httpd's server under `ulimit -n 64` showed it: the connection that filled the table was accepted and answered, and none waited when the line came. Principle 3: when `accept` answers, and for what, is the program's to know, and E.18's `accept` reads as a wait. Ask: E.18 says that at the limit of open files `accept` answers the refusal at once, whether or not a connection waits, and that a connection that comes meanwhile waits in the backlog until a later accept.

29. **`Http.target` reads as none a target `Http.parseRequest` reads and a browser sends, and G.11 names the set `parseRequest` admits as RFC 3986's.** G.11 has `parseRequest` refuse a target byte outside U+0021 to U+007E, "the visible ASCII characters RFC 3986 writes a target in", and `target` read the target as a URI reference, G.12, whose `parse` takes RFC 3986's characters alone. RFC 3986 writes none of `"`, `<`, `>`, `[` and `]` outside an IP literal, `\`, `^`, `` ` ``, `{`, `|` and `}`.

    So `parseRequest` reads `GET /?a[]=1 HTTP/1.1`, `GET /a[b] HTTP/1.1` and `GET /?q={x} HTTP/1.1`, and `target` answers `None` for each; a probe on `ern 0.10.0` shows both. Chromium 156 sends each of the three so, raw: `[` and `]` in a path, and those and `{`, `}`, `|`, `^`, `` ` `` and `\` in a query, as a server logging what it receives shows; it encodes the rest.

    ernest-httpd reads its targets by `Http.target` and answers such a target 400, as RFC 9112 §3.2 allows, so a link to the site with `?a[]=1` is refused. Principle 3: where the two functions part is the program's to know, and the page's sentence reads as though they agree. Principle 1: a server written on `Http` answers 400 to a link a browser follows. Ask: G.11 names the set `parseRequest` admits as the visible ASCII characters and says that `target` answers `None` for one RFC 3986 does not write; and Ernest decides whether `target` reads the characters a browser sends raw, as a server meets them.

30. **E.18 gives no most for what one `Tcp.read` answers, and does not say that what no read has taken holds the far end.** E.18 has `read` answer "what has arrived, at least one byte", and says of a read that times out that the bytes "wait for the next read". Of `write` it says what the host takes and holds; of `read` it says neither how much one read answers nor where the rest waits.

    ernest-httpd's shell reads its socket once each time it has handled the read before (its design §11), so that a client that sends faster than the shell handles is held by the socket and not by the shell's mailbox. A probe on `ern 0.10.0` over plain TCP, a client writing 125-byte pings as fast as it can and reading nothing, showed both: one read answered 9,216 bytes at most while megabytes waited, 309 reads with a mean of 9,083; and once the server read no more, the client's write did not end within its 2 s, after 4.6 MB sent.

    Principle 3: how much one read hands a program, and that a read not made holds the sender, are the program's to know, since it sizes what it holds by the first and rests its bound on the second. Ask: E.18 states the most one read answers, over TCP and over TLS, or that there is none; and that what has arrived and no read has taken waits in the host's and the kernel's buffers for the socket, which hold the far end's writes once they are full.
