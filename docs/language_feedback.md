Line 35 was found on 2026-11-21 against `ern 0.10.0`, while Ernest's close that waits for its bytes was read against the program's closes; lines 36 and 37 were found on 2026-11-23 against `ern 0.11.0+93a13b13`, while the floor's rise timed the program's closes. 35. **Nothing ends a socket and drops what its host holds: after a close or a kill the bytes go on to a far end the program has given up.** E.18 has the bytes a socket has taken sent "while the far end takes them", however the socket ends, and dropped 5 seconds after the far end's last take or 3 minutes after the end. `Tcp` has no function that ends a socket without them.

    A probe on `ern 0.10.0` over ernest-httpd's plain listener on the loopback, `timeouts.write` 2000: a client asks for a 1 MiB file 33 times in one write and reads nothing. The connection closes at its write past the deadline (its design §6), and its place in the caps was free 2.1 s after the request. A client that still read nothing was reset 5.0 s after that. One that then read everything received 4.00 MiB, and one that read 256 kB every 3 s received the 4.00 MiB over 54 s.

    With `limits.connections` 16, three waves of 16 such clients came, each client reading 4 kB a second once it was given up. Every client of every wave was answered, the places of the wave before being free. The node, 107 MiB resident before, stood at 162 after the first wave, 207 after the second and 225 after the third, with 32 descriptors beyond its 18. It was back at 113 MiB 12 s after the clients stopped reading.

    So the program's caps count a connection until it gives the client up, and the host holds the megabytes after, where no cap of the program's counts them. Ernest's session says that its checkout has `close` answer once the bytes are with the kernel or dropped. The place is then held for that wait, 5 seconds against a client that takes nothing and E.18's 3 minutes against one that trickles, and a `kill` leaves the bytes as today.

    Principle 1: a reader predicts that a program which closes a connection at its deadline sends it nothing more. Principle 3: what a program holds for a connection is the program's to know and to end. Ask: `Tcp` gives a function that ends a socket at once and drops what its host holds, the far end reading a reset, and E.18 says so.

36. **A close after a failed TLS handshake waits 5 seconds with nothing to send, and E.18 does not say so.** E.18 has `close` answer at once where "its handshake over TLS has not ended", and otherwise once the socket's bytes and the record that ends the connection are with the kernel, or are dropped 5 seconds after the far end's last take.

    A probe on `ern 0.11.0+93a13b13` over the loopback: a client writes plain text to a listener of `listenTls`. The server's first read fails at once, `Left(Other("TLS server: In state hello ... unsupported_record_type ..."))`, and its `Tcp.close` then answers after 5,001 ms. It does so whether the client has read the server's 7-byte alert or reads nothing, in nine runs of nine, and it answers at once in the two where the client closed first. A `kill` of that socket in place of the close ends its process `Killed` after the same 5,001 ms, the process `Running` meanwhile and its descriptor open, in two runs of two, and at once where the client closed first, though E.18 has `kill` end a socket "as it ends any process".

    ernest-httpd's server shows it: a client that speaks plain HTTP to its port 443 holds its place in the caps 5 seconds past the failed read, and the test of a plain-text client of a TLS listener fails on it. Principle 3: when a close answers is the program's to know, and the page's one clause on a handshake reads as though a failed one answers at once. Ask: E.18 says what a close and a kill after a failed handshake wait for; and Ernest decides whether each ends the socket at once, as a close does where the handshake has not ended.

37. **Over TCP a close that waits answers up to a second after the far end has read the last byte and the close, and E.18 does not say so.** E.18 has `close` answer "once the socket's bytes ... are with the kernel, or the bytes are dropped", and states a lag of up to a second for TLS alone: "Over TLS it reads the close, with the record that ends the connection, when the socket next looks at what the host holds, up to a second later".

    A probe on `ern 0.11.0+93a13b13` over the loopback: a server writes 4 MiB and closes, and its client reads every byte and the close within 4 ms; the server's `Tcp.close` answers 1,000 to 1,001 ms after it began, in five runs of five. Against ernest-httpd's server, a client of an 8 MiB response with `Connection: close` that begins to read 300 ms later reads the close 0.0 ms after the last byte, and the server's close answers after 1,001 ms, in three runs of three.

    So a connection holds its place in ernest-httpd's caps up to a second after a large response, which its client has long had. Principle 3: when a close answers is the program's to know. Ask: E.18 says when a close over TCP answers once the far end has taken everything, and Ernest decides whether it answers then.

38. **`Json.parse` keeps the last of a member named twice, and gives a program no way to tell.** G.6 has an object read as a `Map`, "and where a text names a member twice the last is kept". ernest-httpd's configuration `{"root": "x", "root": "examples/site/root", ...}` serves the second root without a word, and a listener of `"port": 18203, "port": 18202` listens on 18202 (the review's C4 and K8). Ernest's own `ernest.conf` refuses a field named twice. Principle 1: a reader of a file predicts that what it says twice is refused, as `ern` refuses it. Ask: `Json` refuses a member named twice, or answers an object's members in order, so that a program can.

39. **`Os.Command` cannot unset a variable of the inherited environment.** E.23: "It inherits the program's whole environment, with each variable of `environment` set over it ... no variable is unset." ernest-httpd's shell starts a visitor's program with `Os.startTerminal`, and on a developer's machine `SSH_AUTH_SOCK`, `HOME` and `XDG_RUNTIME_DIR` reach it, so the developer's ssh agent is usable from the session (the review's S3). The way around is `env -i` as the command's first word, a second program to do the library's job. Principle 3: what a started program is given is the program's to choose. Ask: a `Command` can start from an empty environment, or unset a variable.

40. **E.23 does not say which signal a kill sends a started program, nor what its process group is sent.** E.23 has the processes still in a program's process group "killed with it". ernest-httpd's wrapper traps `HUP` and `TERM` to remove its container, and the traps did not run, neither at the session's kill `hangup` after a hangup nor when the runtime was killed by `kill -9`, so a container outlived its session (the review's S6, S11, L2 and L14). A program that must clean up cannot learn from the page whether it will have the chance. Principle 3. Ask: E.23 names the signal a kill sends the program and its group, and what they are sent when the runtime dies; and Ernest decides whether a kill sends the termination first and waits a bound.

41. **`Http.reason` names RFC 9110's statuses alone, so a 431 has no phrase.** G.11: "`Http.reason : (Int) -> String // RFC 9110's phrase; "" for a status it does not name`". ernest-httpd answers headers past its limit with `HTTP/1.1 431 ` and a body of `431 ` (the review's K4), and in `ern shell` `List.map([428, 429, 431, 451, 511], Http.reason)` is five empty strings. A server meets 431 at its header limit and 429 at a rate's. Principle 1: a server that writes a standard status expects its standard phrase. Ask: the phrases of IANA's registry, RFC 6585's four and RFC 7725's 451 among them.

42. **The archive of `ern 0.11.0+93a13b13` says nothing of what 0.11 changed.** Its `share/doc/ernest/README.md` is titled "Ernest 0.11.0", and the newest section of its `CHANGES.md` is "0.10.0, 2026-11-10": `ern unpack`, `--package-dir`, `Http.elements` and `String.toAsciiLower` are in the report and in no release note (the review's K21 and D34). A program that raises its floor to 0.11 reads what it takes from the report alone. Principle 3. Ask: an archive carries its release's section, or says that it is a build before the note.

43. **A refusal from the root directory writes an absolute path as a relative one.** Report §11 has a refusal name "the path as resolved". From `/tmp`, `ern run /tmp/nosuch.erc` says `ern run: nosuch.erc: no such file`; from `/` it says `ern run: tmp/nosuch.erc: no such file`. systemd starts a unit in `/`, so each of a unit's refusals reads as a path relative to a directory the operator does not see (the review's K22). Principle 1. Ask: a path is written absolute where the working directory is the root, or always as given.

44. **Every orderly end says two lines of its subscribers, however short the wait.** A SIGTERM to ernest-httpd says `the end waits for 1 subscriber: ErnestHttpd.main` and, a millisecond later, `the subscriber ErnestHttpd.main answered`, at every end (the review's K23). The deployment guide's table explains the first, as said "while it waits"; a user of the program meets the word *subscriber* in no page of the program's. Principle 1: a line that is said every time tells the reader nothing, and reads as a fault. Ask: the line is said once a wait has lasted long enough to be worth a line.

45. **A TLS handshake that fails at the certificate is the host's state and source line.** ernest-httpd's smoke test, dialing a site of a self-signed certificate, is answered `Io.Other("TLS client: In state wait_cert_cr at ssl_handshake.erl:2306 generated CLIENT ALERT: Fatal - Bad Certificate\n selfsigned_peer")` (the review's K24): the cause is the last word, after a line feed, behind a state and a file of OTP's, and the program can match on none of it. Principle 1 and principle 3. Ask: an error of `Io`'s own for a certificate that does not verify, with the host's reason in it.

46. **A head's read answers `Http.Error`, whose `BodyTooLarge` it never answers.** G.11's `readRequest` answers a `SocketError` over `Error = Malformed | LineTooLong | HeadersTooLarge | BodyTooLarge`, and only a body's read meets a body's limit. ernest-httpd's connection must give the case that cannot come a clause, `Protocol(_) -> 400` (the review's C13 and C22). Principle 3: a type that holds what cannot come hides what can. Ask: a head's read answers an error of the head's alone.

47. **`Os.read` under a terminal answers `Stdout` alone, and its type keeps `Stderr`.** E.23: "The program's standard output and standard error come merged through the terminal, so `read` answers `Stdout(bytes)` alone and last `Exited(status)`." ernest-httpd's session reads a program started by `startTerminal` and gives `Stderr` a clause that cannot run (the review's C13 and C23). Principle 3. Ask: a terminal's read answers a type of its own, without `Stderr`.

48. **`Uri.decode` answers `Optional` over a path that `Http.target` has made whole.** G.11 has `Http.target` encode a `%` that does not begin an encoding, and G.12 has `decode` answer `None` for "a `%` not followed by two hexadecimal digits" alone, so a segment of the path a target gives always decodes. ernest-httpd's `Static.segment` answers its `None` with a 400 that cannot come (the review's C13 and C24). Principle 3. Ask: `Http.target`'s path comes as its segments decoded, or a decode of a path that `target` gave answers bytes.

49. **A named constructor is no function value, so a wrapper of `Http.Header` is written in four modules.** Report §5: "A named constructor is neither; it appears only in construction syntax." ernest-httpd writes `fn header(name, value) = Http.Header(=name, =value)` in `src/connection.ern`, `src/hosts.ern`, `src/static.ern` and `src/shell.ern` (the review's C15, C25 and S13). Principle 2: one form for a header, written once. Ask: a named constructor taken as a function of its fields in order, or `Http` gives `header(name, value)`.

50. **A declaration is private or exported, and nothing between, so tests copy their fixtures.** Report §9: "`fn parse` ... is exported as `Net.Http.parse` when marked `export`; otherwise it is private to its module." ernest-httpd's tests copy a certificate and its key, 27 lines, into two modules, and a helper `next` into three; `Smoke` exports six test helpers, which `ern doc` shows as its interface (the review's C15 and C26). Principle 2. Ask: a declaration that a module's tests and its package's tests see and its users do not.

51. **`Bytes` has no function that combines two byte strings by exclusive or, so a WebSocket mask is applied a byte at a time.** E.20 has no such function. ernest-httpd's `WebSocket.maskedFrom` masks and unmasks a frame's payload one byte at a time, over `Bytes.toList`, the key's bytes turned at each step (the review's C27 and L18). It is written in the program, as `CLAUDE.md` allows a function the library lacks, and this line names it. Principle 5: a protocol's every frame pays for a loop the library could hold. Ask: `Bytes.xor`, a mask of a repeated key over bytes, or `Bytes.map`.

52. **`Http` reads a list header's elements and leaves the test of a token to every caller.** G.11's `elements` answers the elements as written, and RFC 9110 compares a token in ASCII case. ernest-httpd writes `List.any(Http.elements(headers, name), fn(element) = String.toAsciiLower(element) == token)` in `src/connection.ern` and `src/shell.ern` (the review's S14), and its design states the rule four times. Principle 2. Ask: `Http.lists(headers, name, token)`, the test RFC 9110 reads.

53. **E.18 says nothing of TLS renegotiation.** E.18 has a TLS listener of a certificate and a key: "There are no options." Against ernest-httpd's TLS listener, an OpenSSL client on TLS 1.2 renegotiated once and was refused the second time (the review's S16). Nothing of the program rests on it, and the program cannot choose it. Principle 3. Ask: E.18 says what a listener of `listenTls` does with a client's renegotiation.

54. **A call waits for its answer by scanning its caller's whole mailbox, and §6.6 states no cost.** A probe made 1000 `Address.call`s to an echo process from a caller with 0, 10,000, 20,000, 40,000 and 80,000 messages waiting, and they took 2, 65, 128, 250 and 513 ms. Every library function over a call carries the cost: `Os.resize`, `Tcp.read` and `Tcp.write` among them. ernest-httpd's session answers each resize a visitor sends with `Os.resize`, so a flood of resizes slows each one as the backlog grows: 80,000 took 21 s, and the session's alarm and the server's stop waited behind them (the review's L1 and L13). A reader who knows the host predicts a wait that passes over the messages queued before the request. Principles 1 and 3. Ask: a call's wait passes over what was queued before its request, or §6.6 states the cost.

55. **Nothing bounds a mailbox or paces a send, so a program paces each channel by hand.** ernest-httpd paces four channels with one request for each answer: the listener's acceptor (`Accept`), the shell's reader (`Read`), the session's reader (`Read`, against the shell's `Written`) and its writer (`Write` and `Wrote`). The one channel it does not pace, a shell's to its session, lets one visitor's resizes grow the server's memory by about 60 MiB a second (the review's L1 and L15). A mailbox's growth is invisible at the `send` that makes it. Principle 3. Ask: a bound on what a mailbox holds, or a form of pacing the report states.

56. **A helper ends with its owner only where it monitors it, so three helpers carry their owner's end.** Report §6.9 has a spawned process end with no other, and the prelude has `spawn` and `spawnMonitored` alone. ernest-httpd's shell's reader and its session's reader and writer each monitor their owner, each with an `...Ended(Down)` constructor, a clause for it and a comment citing §6.9 (the review's L16). Principle 2: one form for a helper's life, written three times. Ask: a spawn whose process ends with the process that spawned it.

57. **The library has no WebSocket, so a program writes RFC 6455.** G.11's `Http` reads and writes HTTP/1.1 and offers no upgrade. ernest-httpd's `src/web_socket.ern` holds RFC 6455's framing, its close codes and its accept value in 550 lines before its tests, written as a library of Ernest's is (its design §10; the review's L17). A published specification is a library's work. Principles 2 and 5. Ask: a `WebSocket` beside `Http`.
